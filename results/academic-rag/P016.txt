ABSTRACT Bearing elements play important roles in mechanical machining. Efficient monitoring and
detection of bearing faults are critical for ensuring motor reliability. Deep learning techniques, particularly
those that use convolutional neural networks, have emerged as prominent solutions for achieving high
accuracy in bearing fault classification. However, the computational complexities of these models render
them unsuitable for use in resource-constrained devices. In this study, we explored the application of a
combination of multiple techniques, including knowledge distillation, layer fusion, and quantization-aware
training, to develop lightweight models that accelerate inference speed while maintaining high accuracy.
In addition, acoustic emission signals containing bearing health information were preprocessed using a
short-time Fourier transform and converted into grayscale spectrograms before being fed into a neural
network. The experimental results indicate that the proposed method can significantly reduce the inference
latency and memory footprint while achieving high accuracy for different bearing fault types at variable
rotational speeds.

I. INTRODUCTION
Industrial machinery serves as the backbone of modern
manufacturing and production processes, in which numer-
ous rotating and reciprocating components collaborate to
ensure smooth operation. Among these components, bearings
play an indispensable role in supporting mechanical rotation,
reducing friction, and extending equipment lifespan. How-
ever, bearing faults are the most common cause of severe
machinery failures, accounting for 50% of such incidents [1],
leading to unplanned downtimes and substantial financial
losses if not promptly detected and addressed [2]. Therefore,
the early detection and accurate diagnosis of bearing faults are
of paramount importance. Historically, manual inspections
have served as the primary line of defense, relying on visual

schedules, which can be time-consuming and susceptible
to human error. Although these conventional methods can
uncover certain faults, they often hinge on human expertise
and may overlook subtle early stage defects. Consequently,
automated diagnostic systems are increasingly being utilized
to bridge these gaps by employing sensor-driven data acquisi-
tion and signal-processing techniques. Vibration signals have
traditionally dominated this field owing to well-established
analytical methods and strong track records in fault detec-
tion [3], [4]. These signals mostly lie in the lower-frequency
ranges and capture the global resonant response of a structure
or rotating element. By collecting and analyzing vibration
data from sensors mounted on or near bearings, engineers and
researchers can monitor subtle changes in the signal char-
acteristics that often precede a full-scale malfunction. The
ability to detect incipient defects such as spalls on the inner

or outer race, damaged rolling elements, or cage instabilities,
makes vibration-based monitoring an essential component of

ity of vibration-based methods, their real-world implementa-

from gearboxes, shafts, and other adjacent rotating compo-

thresholding techniques that adjust to changing operational
conditions. In certain instances, sophisticated denoising and
feature-extraction algorithms are required to prevent false
alarms and missed detections [5].
Acoustic emission (AE) signals are gaining traction
because of their nonintrusive nature and capacity to highlight
distinctive bearing fault patterns, particularly in environ-
ments where vibration measurement is more challenging.
AE signals are high-frequency stress waves generated by the
rapid release of strain energy within a material or machine
component, which typically occurs during crack initiation,
crack propagation, or other defect-related phenomena [6].
This high-frequency domain is often less contaminated by
ambient machinery or environmental noise which predom-
inantly clusters at lower frequencies. Tang et al. concluded
that AE signals exhibit a relatively high signal-to-noise ratio
compared with vibration signals [7]. Consequently, AE sig-
nals can provide a clearer signature for diagnostic purposes,
thereby reducing the complexity of filtering and feature
extraction. Furthermore, AE-based systems can be designed
for near-real-time monitoring because transient events are
detected and processed quickly after they occur. Although
advanced data-acquisition electronics and high-speed sam-
pling are typically necessary, modern digital hardware has
made it feasible to implement real-time algorithms that can
recognize AE bursts almost instantaneously.
With the proliferation of artificial intelligence (AI), which
encompasses classical machine learning techniques, such
as support vector machines (SVMs) and random forests,
as well as state-of-the-art deep learning paradigms, such as
convolutional neural networks (CNNs), its applicability has
permeated a diverse array of disciplines. Nguyen and Lee pro-
posed a nonlinear equalizer using a CNN for holographic data
storage systems and achieved superior accuracy compared
with traditional equalizers [8]. Bui et al. used Cloud-Net
and Change-Net for change detection in satellite-based Earth
observation applications [9]. In the field of fault diagno-
sis, deep learning techniques have been widely utilized as
extensions of data-driven approaches [10], [11]. A CNN
model can automatically discover patterns in AE signals that
correlate with specific fault types or severity levels. These
models can be generalized beyond the conditions under which
they are trained to identify subtle early stage faults and
issue timely warnings by leveraging large training datasets
spanning diverse operating conditions. Anbalagan et al. pro-
posed a physics-informed deep learning approach for bearing
fault detection under different motor working conditions [12].
Wan et al. used an improved 2D LeNet-5 network to address
the problems of low recognition accuracy, slow convergence
speed, and weak generalization ability associated with tradi-
tional LeNet-5 [13].
However, a notable limitation of CNN-based methods in
the field of bearing fault diagnosis is the high computa-
tional resource overhead compared with common traditional
methods [14]. This issue has been investigated in several
studies. These studies have employed custom lightweight
CNNs tailored for edge computing or embedded devices
with constrained resources. However, most of these investiga-
tions have concentrated on a single aspect or a specific type
of technique, failing to comprehensively address the multi-
faceted challenges encountered in real-world scenarios. First,
many compression methods have been validated on con-
trolled datasets (e.g., CWRU). However, they degrade under
real-world conditions characterized by noise, nonstationary
speeds, or imbalanced fault distributions. Second, although
a few studies have incorporated compression techniques,
they continue to impose substantial computational demands
during the data-processing phase. Pham et al. applied the
pruning technique to MobileNetV2 at various scaling rates to
demonstrate its feasibility and reliability for use in embed-
ded systems targeted at bearing fault diagnosis. However,
they did not address critical aspects such as the dataset
memory footprint or the complexity associated with the
data-processing technique [15]. Pham et al. also utilized a
neural architecture search and shared weights to achieve a
significant reduction in the number of multiply-accumulate
(MAC) operations, parameters, and overall inference latency.
However, this approach remains constrained by its high
power consumption, memory usage, and computationally
intensive data-processing flow [16]. Furthermore, bench-
marks often neglect deployment metrics such as the energy
consumption and latency of microcontrollers, favoring theo-
retical compression ratios. Yang et al. developed an improved
knowledge-distillation method to address the problems of low
computational accuracy and challenging hardware deploy-
ment in practical engineering applications of rolling bearing
fault diagnosis [17]. Despite its promising design, the lack
of demonstrated deployment in embedded systems raises
concerns regarding its real-world feasibility.
An alternative approach to lightweight classification
involves the use of one-dimensional CNNs (1D-CNNs) [18].
Although 1D-CNNs offer reduced model complexity, they
are generally less effective for handling nonstationary AE
signals and tend to underperform in terms of classification
accuracy compared with 2D-CNNs. Binarized neural net-
works (BNNs) are a promising approach for real-time infer-
ence in edge computing scenarios. They replace expensive
floating-point multiplications with simple bitwise operations
such as XNOR and bit counting, leading to substantial gains

neuromorphic hardware to achieve low-power consumption
and remain a major challenge in training frameworks because
of their nondifferentiable nature. A more recent advancement
in this domain is the adoption of hardware-aware neural
architecture search (HW-NAS), which automatically designs
CNN architectures optimized not only for high accuracy but
also for accelerating the inference speed, reducing memory
usage, and lowering the number of floating-point operations,
enabling deployment on various target platforms, especially
ultra-low-resource microcontrollers [24], [25], [26], [27].
Despite these advantages, the computational complexity of
search algorithms is a barrier to their practical adoption,
particularly in scenarios requiring rapid model deployment
and design cycles.
Although recent advances in bearing fault diagnosis for
edge deployment have made notable progress, significant
limitations remain in addressing the diverse operational
complexities encountered in real-world applications. Many
studies have focused on model compression techniques, such
as network pruning, quantization, and architecture optimiza-
tion, to reduce computational complexity. However, these
efforts often overlook the crucial aspects of robust and
efficient data preprocessing. Furthermore, many of the pro-
posed methods have not been rigorously validated through
actual deployment in embedded systems, instead relying
on evaluations conducted in desktop-based environments.
This disconnection between algorithmic development and
practical implementation raises concerns regarding the true
readiness of current approaches for real-world industrial
applications, particularly in highly resource-constrained edge
scenarios.
To address these challenges, this study emphasizes the
optimization of both the signal-processing and inference
stages of a bearing fault diagnosis framework that leverages
2D single-channel spectrograms derived from AE signals in
conjunction with hybrid compression techniques to achieve
a balance between diagnostic accuracy and computational
efficiency. The proposed approach is designed for edge AI
environments and demonstrates the potential for real-time
operation on central processing units (CPUs) rather than
relying on graphics processing units (GPUs). Specifically,
a short-time Fourier transform (STFT) is employed to analyze
the bearing signals at variable rotational speeds. This enables
a time–frequency representation of AE signals, effectively
capturing transient and nonstationary features that are critical
for accurate fault diagnosis, while offering a well-balanced
trade-off between diagnostic accuracy and computational
efficiency. To further optimize data processing, we con-
vert the three-channel spectrograms into single-channel
grayscale representations. This dimension reduction signifi-
cantly decreases the storage requirements of the dataset while
maintaining high classification accuracy. Consequently, the
number of input channels in the CNN architecture is reduced,
resulting in a lighter inference pipeline.
In addition, we incorporated several model optimization
strategies to minimize the memory footprint and enhance
the inference speed. First, knowledge distillation (KD)
was employed to transfer learned representations from a
high-capacity teacher model to a compact student model.
By aligning the output logits or intermediate feature distri-
butions, KD enables the student model to approximate the
teacher’s performance while significantly reducing the num-
ber of parameters and overall computational cost. Second,
layer fusion is applied to combine adjacent linear operations,
such as convolution, followed by batch normalization and
activation, into a single computational unit. This transforma-
tion simplifies the execution graph, minimizes the memory
access overhead, and reduces the inference latency, partic-
ularly for hardware accelerators and embedded platforms.
Third, we adopted quantization-aware training (QAT) to
prepare a model for low-bit inference while maintaining accu-
racy. Unlike post-training quantization, QAT simulates the
quantization effects during training, allowing the model to
adapt its weights. As a result, the model achieves substantial
reductions in model size and inference time with minimal
performance loss. The main contributions of this study are
as follows:
1) The experimental testbed is specifically designed to
collect AE signals directly, thereby eliminating the
need for extensive denoising procedures that are com-
monly required when acquiring vibration signals.
2) We employ an efficient STFT to convert raw AE signals
into 2D spectrograms, enabling the time–frequency
representation of nonstationary signal characteristics.
To validate the effectiveness of our preprocessing
strategy, we conducted a comparative analysis against
other widely used time–frequency analysis techniques.
Subsequently, the generated spectrograms were trans-
formed into single-channel grayscale images, which
significantly reduced both computational complexity
and dataset storage requirements. This transforma-
tion streamlines the input pipeline of the model while

II. RELATED WORK
Fourier transform (FT) is a basic digital signal-processing
(DSP) technique that converts time-domain signals into
frequency-domain representations. A primary advantage of
FT is its ability to decompose complex AE signals into
discrete frequency components and efficiently isolate them,
enabling precise fault identification [28]. Furthermore, the
computational efficiency of the fast Fourier transform (FFT)
algorithm facilitates real-time monitoring, which is essen-
tial for industrial applications requiring immediate fault
detection. Despite its strengths, FT faces limitations when
analyzing nonstationary AE signals because their frequency
content evolves over time.
Other methods can offer enhanced time–frequency anal-
ysis and improve transient fault detection. STFT is an
extension of FT. It applies FT to segmented portions of a sig-
nal using a sliding time window to generate a time–frequency
spectrogram [29]. Wavelet transform (WT) uses mother
wavelets of localized waveforms with adjustable scales to
decompose signals into multiresolution components, thereby
addressing the shortcomings of STFT [30]. Hilbert–Huang
transform (HHT) combines empirical mode decomposition
(EMD) and Hilbert transform to analyze nonlinear and
nonstationary signals. EMD adaptively decomposes AE sig-
nals into intrinsic mode functions (IMF), from which the
instantaneous frequencies are derived [31]. HHT is particu-
larly effective for diagnosing faults under variable loads or
speeds; however, it is susceptible to mode mixing and noise
sensitivity [32].
Similarly, an advanced DSP technique, known as the con-
stant Q transform (CQT), was developed to analyze signals

In addition to these techniques, recent advancements
have focused on transforming 1-D time-series data into
2-D image representations to leverage the powerful feature
extraction capabilities of CNNs. For example, Lian et al.
proposed a novel time-series data-imaging scheme that con-
verts raw vibration signals into images using various DSP
methods [36], thereby enhancing diagnostic performance
by capturing features across multiple domains within a
unified image representation. Similarly, frameworks such
as CFI-LFENet utilize cross-domain fusion imaging with
lightweight feature-enhanced networks [37], achieving high
diagnostic accuracy in identifying various fault types while
reducing the computational complexity.
Beyond transform-based methods, recent research has
increasingly focused on stationary–nonstationary disentan-
glement techniques to improve the interpretability of fault
diagnosis models. These methods aim to separate signals into
components that capture long-term invariant patterns (sta-
tionary features) and those that reflect dynamic fault-related
variations (nonstationary features). Although traditional sig-
nal decomposition methods have been used for this purpose,
more recent studies have leveraged deep learning-based
representations for improved performance and flexibility.
Yu et al. [38] proposed a model for industrial nonstationary
fault detection using multiscale content learning to disen-
tangle features in the data. Yu et al. [39] introduced a
mutual stacked autoencoder architecture to enhance local
information and address the challenges of multivariable fault
detection in complex industrial environments.

Neural network compression has emerged as a critical
research area for addressing the computational, memory,
and energy constraints associated with the deployment of
deep learning models on resource-limited devices. Pruning
involves removing redundant parameters while preserving
model performance. Early work by Han et al. popularized
magnitude-based weight pruning, which iteratively trims
weights with minimal absolute values [40]. Subsequent stud-
ies introduced structured pruning, which removes all neurons,
filters, and channels to maintain hardware-friendly sparsity
patterns [41], [42].
Ding et al. utilized adaptive pruning to avoid the repetitive
and cumbersome processes characteristic of traditional prun-
ing techniques for bearing fault diagnosis [43]. Quantization
helps reduce the numerical precision of the weights and
activations. Binary [44] and ternary networks [45] achieve
extreme compression, but often experience accuracy degra-
dation. Li et al. applied k-means quantization to reduce the
computational cost of the model significantly while ensur-
ing unchanged accuracy [46]. To address the performance
degradation in low-bit regimes, Chung et al. proposed a
QAT combined with post-training quantization to improve
the training convergence speed and minimize the num-
ber of parameters [47]. Another compression technique is
KD, which transforms knowledge from complex models
into lightweight variants. Ma et al. used a hybrid model
framework that combines KD and deep forests for fault clas-
sification [48]. Despite this progress, key limitations hinder
industrial adoption. Therefore, data processing and neural
model compression techniques must be considered to address
these barriers.

deploy advanced deep learning models on embedded devices
to achieve real-time inference in resource-constrained envi-
ronments. The methodology is structured into two phases:
offline and online. Fig. 1 shows a diagram of the proposed

C. KNOWLEDGE DISTILLATION
KD is a technique introduced to transfer the knowledge
learned by a large, complex model (often referred to as the
teacher) to a smaller, more efficient model (the student). This
is a type of transfer-learning method. First popularized by
Hinton et al., KD has become a powerful methodology for
compressing deep neural networks without a substantial loss
in predictive accuracy [51]. Intuitively, the teacher model
output softens the target distribution for the student, guiding
it to learn both the correct labels and interclass relationships,
which are often captured in teacher-output probabilities even
for incorrect classes. By exploiting these softened probabil-
ities, the student model can achieve a better generalization
than if it is trained purely on hard one-hot labels.
One critical advantage of KD is that the student model can
have far fewer parameters than the teacher model while main-
taining a comparable level of accuracy. This process is akin
to model compression; however, rather than simply pruning
weights or applying quantization, the method leverages richer
‘‘dark knowledge’’ in the teacher’s output distribution. This
subtle interclass information acts as a form of regularization,
helping students to generalize better than from purely hard
labels.

This integration enables the model to adjust its parameters

Fake quantization in the forward pass is a technique used

F. CNN MODELS AND TRAINING ALGORITHM
In computer vision applications, including object detection,
semantic segmentation, and fault diagnosis, CNNs have gar-
nered significant attention owing to their high accuracy and
ability to automatically learn discriminative features from
raw or transformed signals. CNN architectures for bearing
fault diagnosis can range from lightweight custom mod-
els to well-known backbones such as AlexNet or ResNet.

ResNet-50 is a deep CNN architecture that belongs to the
ResNet family, and was introduced by Kaiming et al. [52].
The ResNet framework is a breakthrough in addressing the
degradation problem that arises when very deep neural net-
works are trained, and the phenomenon in which adding
more layers can lead to higher training errors rather than
improved accuracy. By introducing residual (skip) connec-
tions, ResNet-50 can learn more efficiently even with a
substantial number of layers. This innovation ultimately
opened the door to the construction of neural networks with
hundreds or even thousands of layers, without suffering from
vanishing or exploding gradients to the same degree as prior
architectures. One notable feature of ResNet-50 is its use as a
bottleneck. Instead of repeatedly applying 3×3 convolutions,
each block typically employs a 1×1 →3×3 →1×1 stack
(Fig. 7). This strategy reduces the computational cost and
number of parameters compared with the naïve approach of
stacking multiple 3 × 3 convolutions.
Despite its high accuracy and ease of training in classi-
fication tasks, the ResNet-50 architecture is relatively large
and computationally intensive, which makes its deployment
on mobile or embedded devices with limited resources chal-
lenging. MobileNet, introduced by Howard at Google [53],
addresses this gap by tailoring the CNN architecture specif-
ically for resource-constrained environments and striking a
balance between the model size, speed, and accuracy. Hence,
in this study, we selected MobileNetV2 and MobileNetV3-
Large as lightweight CNN architectures for the student
models.
For the training algorithm in the offline phase, we used an
Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.001 (stages 1 and
2) and 0.0001 (stage 3) and a batch size of 32. The criterion
used to calculate the loss function was cross-entropy. We also
employed a learning rate scheduler that adjusts the learning
rate over time to balance the rapid initial learning with stable
convergence. The scheduler monitors the validation loss and
when improvements in this metric stagnate, it automatically
reduces the learning rate, allowing the model to further refine
its weights.

3) EDGE DEPLOYMENT
To validate the practical efficiency of the proposed method,
the inference latency of the final MobileNetV2 (input reso-
lution: 32 × 32 pixels) deployed on an embedded platform
was evaluated. We compared our method with pruning-based
techniques from [15], which reduced model complexity by
removing redundant parameters at various scaling levels (0.2,
0.1, and 0.01). As shown in Fig. 13, our method achieved
a CNN inference latency of 100.26 ms, with an extremely
lightweight signal-processing time of just 1.2 ms, resulting
in a total latency of 101.46 ms. This outperforms all pruning-
based models, including the most aggressively compressed
model at scale 0.01, which still provides a higher total
latency of 118.2 ms due to its costly signal-processing stage.
Whereas the pruned models in [15] employed the EEMD
and CQT frameworks, which demand up to 29.2 ms for
signal preprocessing, our method benefits from a much more
efficient STFT-based transformation and grayscale image
representation. Moreover, our method achieves a competitive
accuracy of 98.7%, compared to 99.17% at scale 0.2 and
98.95% at scale 0.01, as reported in [15]. These findings
clearly demonstrate that our approach offers a superior bal-
ance between inference speed, accuracy, and computational
efficiency, making it suitable for embedded and edge-based
fault diagnosis systems, where processing power and latency
are tightly constrained.
V. CONCLUSION
In this study, we propose a novel approach that utilizes
the computationally efficient processing of AE signals com-
bined with a hybrid neural network compression technique
to demonstrate the feasibility of deploying bearing fault
diagnosis across variable rotational speeds in embedded sys-
tems. The proposed method achieved inference times and
memory footprints suitable for highly resource-constrained
environments such as Raspberry Pi board platforms, while
preserving high diagnostic accuracy. Nevertheless, this study
highlights several points that merit further investigation in
future research. First, the dataset used in this study was
collected under controlled, idealized conditions and may not
fully reflect the challenges encountered in real-world indus-
trial environments, where nonstationary operating conditions
often lead to significant variations in data distributions.
Second, the data preprocessing and inference processes
were conducted independently rather than as an inte-
grated pipeline, potentially limiting the evaluation of the
effectiveness of the method under practical deployment
scenarios. In addition, the power consumption remains a
critical factor when implementing deep learning models on
edge devices, particularly in energy-constrained environ-
ments. In future work, we plan to explore energy-efficient
approaches that will enable the deployment of our models
to ultra-resource-constrained microcontrollers. Specifically,
we intend to target low-bit platforms, such as 16-bit or 32-bit
CPUs, operating under battery-powered conditions. Through
these efforts, we hope to advance the practical applicabil-
ity and scalability of our approach in real-world industrial
environments.
