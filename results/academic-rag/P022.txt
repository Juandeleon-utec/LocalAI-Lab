Abstract—Industrial three-phase induction motors are critical
to manufacturing operations, yet their failure can cause costly
downtime and maintenance overhead. This work introduces a
machine learning framework tailored for fault diagnosis and
severity assessment in such motors, using synthetic data that
replicates six realistic electromagnetic and mechanical fault con-
ditions. The system employs synchronized vibration and current
signals, and evaluates several classifiers—including LSTM, GRU,
TCN, RNN, and Random Forest—under varying industrial noise
profiles such as phase jitter, frequency drift, and transient spikes.
Experimental results demonstrate that time-series models con-
sistently outperform classical approaches in noisy environments.
For deployment on resource-constrained edge devices, structured
pruning and quantization are applied to reduce model size
and latency. Beyond motor fault classification, the framework
also detects and visualizes approximately spherical clusters to
comprehend fault severity levels. The proposed system enables
scalable fault monitoring and low-latency predictive maintenance
optimized for embedded industrial environments.
Index Terms—Edge AI, edge computing, induction motor,
industrial IoT, predictive maintenance, synthetic data
I. INTRODUCTION
Three-phase induction motors are foundational to industrial
operations, manufacturing processes and transportation infras-
tructure worldwide [1]. They are used to power a vast array of
applications due to their robustness, ease of maintenance, and
high efficiency. Given their crucial role, their uninterrupted
operation is critical to production continuity. Even minor
performance degradations can result in significant financial
losses, production downtime, and potential safety hazards.
Consequently, there is growing interest in predictive mainte-
nance strategies that can detect faults early, reduce unplanned
outages, and improve overall operational efficiency [2].
Traditionally, diagnostic methods for industrial motors have
relied on scheduled maintenance checks, simple threshold-
based monitoring approaches and human expertise for analysis
[3]. While these approaches provide some fault coverage, they
are often reactive, lack sensitivity to early-stage anomalies,

depend heavily on domain expertise and are prone to hu-

acquisition and computational capabilities, machine learning
(ML) techniques have emerged as a powerful alternative for
automating fault detection. ML algorithms can extract subtle
fault signatures, adapt to varying operating conditions, and
offer more accurate and timely diagnostics [4]. By analyz-
ing patterns in operational data, these algorithms can detect
anomalies and infer the health state of an induction motor more
systematically and with greater precision and with minimized
human intervention [3].
Despite their promise, training robust ML models for in-
dustrial environments presents several challenges. First, the
development of robust models requires diverse, high-quality
datasets representing various fault scenarios. However, col-
lecting labeled data under all possible fault conditions is
expensive, time-consuming, and often unsafe, especially if it
involves deliberately inducing faults in operational machin-
ery [5]. Second, industrial signals are subject to high noise
levels, phase jitters, and intermittent interruptions resulting
from voltage instabilities or environmental factors. These
noise sources can hamper the performance of conventional
algorithms, which struggle to extract reliable features under
real-world conditions [6]. Third, real-time diagnostics demand
continuous processing of high-throughput sensor streams, of-
ten on resource-constrained edge devices with limited memory,
compute power, and energy availability. These constraints
necessitate lightweight and compressed ML models suitable
for embedded deployments for onsite monitoring [7].
In this study, we present a comprehensive framework for
fault diagnosis in industrial three-phase induction motors,
covering the entire diagnostic pipeline from data collection
to deployment in resource-limited embedded systems. Our
approach focuses on six distinct electromagnetic and me-
chanical faults and evaluating multiple architectures under
varying industrial noise conditions. To enhance real-world
applicability, we apply compression techniques to increase ef-
ficiency and support embedded deployment. Furthermore, we
extend our analysis beyond fault classification by employing
cluster visualizations to assess fault severity levels, providing
a holistic understanding of fault progression and motor health.

II. LITERATURE REVIEW
Machine learning-based motor fault diagnosis has evolved
significantly, offering advancements in predictive maintenance
strategies for industrial three-phase induction motors. By au-
tomating feature extraction and improving classification accu-
racy, ML techniques have outperformed traditional rule-based
and threshold-driven approaches. Classical models such as
Support Vector Machines (SVM) [8] and modern deep learning
models like Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) [9] have
been successfully applied to machine condition monitoring and
fault diagnosis, with CNNs demonstrating efficacy in process-
ing vibrational spectroscopic data and eliminating the need for
manual preprocessing. Similarly, deep transfer learning has
been employed to improve diagnostic precision. A transfer
learning-based framework is proposed in [10] that converts
sensor data into time-frequency representations, allowing for
superior classification performance across different mechanical
components.
One of the primary challenges in machine learning for
fault diagnosis is the availability of sufficient labeled data.
Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) have been explored
as a viable solution for augmenting limited datasets. In
[11], it is demonstrated how GANs could synthesize high-
fidelity training data, thereby improving model generalization.
Additionally, hybrid approaches that integrate physics-based
modeling with deep learning have been proposed to enhance
decision-making processes. A physics-informed neural net-
works is proposed in [12] that leverage domain knowledge
to refine fault detection models. Since motor fault signals
are inherently sequential, time-series analysis plays a crucial
role in fault diagnosis. Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs)
have been widely adopted to capture temporal dependencies
in signal measurements. The authors in [13] reviewed vari-
ous RNN architectures, including Long Short-Term Memory
(LSTM) networks, emphasizing their ability to model transient
fault patterns in motor vibration and current signatures. These
architectures have proven effective in diagnosing faults even
in noisy environments.
Recent advancements in Embedded AI have enabled real-
time fault diagnosis on resource-constrained edge computing
devices. A comprehensive review of AI integration is provided
in [7] for embedded systems, demonstrating the feasibility
of deploying sophisticated machine learning models in low-
power environments. This innovation is particularly beneficial
for industrial settings where continuous monitoring is required
with minimal computational overhead. Attention-based archi-
tecture has been increasingly explored to enhance the detection
of localized signal irregularities. In [14], an attention-driven
fault diagnosis system is explained that selectively weighed
relevant signal segments, thereby improving classification ac-
curacy. Zhou et al. [15] further expanded on this concept
by integrating attention mechanisms with hybrid CNN-MLP
models, effectively detecting sporadic and noise-obscured fault
signatures in motor systems.
A. Research Gap
Although the aforementioned studies mention significant
contributions, it is important to highlight that much of the
existing studies focus on specific model types under con-
trolled conditions. Usually, a narrowly selected aspect of
motor behavior is examined, such as stator failures, or rotor
breakage analysis. Also, studies more often utilize a binary
classification approach to assess if the motor is healthy or
faulty, instead of incorporating more extensive multiclass sit-
uations. Another important need is to comprehensively model
the noise conditions, since results derived from controlled
laboratory settings may not always correspond with actual
industrial environments. Finally, it can be stated that models
that perform well frequently remain untested in embedded
systems or constrained deployment environments, which are
the most crucial in industrial setups.
B. Our Contribution
Our study addresses identified gaps by examining six multi-
class cases covering electromagnetic and mechanical faults.
The evaluation of the developed models is conducted within
a comprehensive noise modeling framework that involves
gradually increasing frequency drift, phase jitter, background
Gaussian noise, and amplitude distortions. This approach
discusses the progression of model performance as the elec-
tromagnetic environment becomes increasingly complex. We
then apply quantization and pruning to optimize Edge AI
feasibility. Also, our work goes beyond mere classification,
and provides approximately spherical clustering results of
spectral and frequency features, representing the severity and
progression of faults.
III. METHODOLOGY
A. Synthetic Data Generation
This work builds upon our prior study [16], where we de-
veloped a high-fidelity synthetic signal generation framework
for replicating the behavior of industrial three-phase induction
motors under multiple fault conditions. The dataset based on
real motor data [17] models a four-pole, three-phase induction
motor (AGM2E 112 M 4a) operating at the European standard
of 50 Hz, with a rated voltage of 400 V and a nominal speed of
1455 RPM. Such motors are commonly deployed in conveyor
systems, HVAC compressors, and water treatment pumps due
to their robustness and efficiency.
In this study, six operational states are considered: Healthy,
Rotor Bar Defect, Stator Winding Fault, Current Overload,
Insufficient Current, and Mechanical Fault. Each fault type
represents a distinct electrical or mechanical disruption, lead-
ing to characteristic patterns in current and vibration signals.
These effects are observable in both time and frequency
domains [18] These fault-specific signal characteristics are
outlined in Table I.

brid approach combining statistical modeling, signal trans-
formation, and domain-specific waveform formulations, as

and 50% faulty (120,000) samples distributed among the

validation set follows the same class distribution as the training
data. To enable class-fair evaluation and clear confusion-
matrix interpretation, separate balanced test datasets of 60,000
samples (10,000 per class) were generated under both low- and
high-noise conditions, ensuring no overlap with the training
data. Each test sample similarly contained tri-axial vibration
and three-phase current signals at a 100 kHz sampling rate,
enabling unbiased benchmarking of the classification models.
B. Feature Engineering
To enable efficient and real-time classification, we apply a
feature engineering pipeline that transforms the raw signals
into compact, informative descriptors. Each synchronized cur-
rent and vibration stream is sampled at 100 kHz and divided
into overlapping sliding windows of 1000 samples, targeting
transient signatures rather than complete waveform cycles.
For every window, a total of 78 time-domain and frequency-
domain features are computed across the three accelerometer

include root mean square (RMS), standard deviation, skew-

The resulting feature matrix forms a structured, low-

industrial motor fault diagnosis, we examined both classical
and time-series-based approaches across both low and high
noise levels. The primary objective was to assess the capability
of each architecture to handle noisy and sequential signal
features typical of real-world industrial environments.
We considered five model types in total. Random Forest
(RF) was selected as the baseline classical approach, operating
on individual feature vectors without incorporating temporal
context. In contrast, the remaining four models—Recurrent

D. Optimization for Low-Power Constrained Environments
To address the resource constraints often encountered in in-
dustrial environments [22], advanced compression techniques
for Edge AI has gained momentum [23] to allow inference
directly on local, lower-power boards. To support real-time
fault diagnosis in industrial environments, we applied em-
bedded machine learning optimization techniques to reduce
model size and inference latency. These optimizations enable
the deployment of trained models on edge AI platforms with
limited computational and memory resources. An example
server-to-edge transformation diagram can be seen in Fig. 3
The methodology focuses on two key techniques: structured
pruning and post-training quantization. Structured pruning

Toolkit (TFMOT), where low-importance channels/units were

sity schedule. After pruning, the corresponding channels/units
were physically removed and the model was fine-tuned to

Following pruning, post-training quantization (PTQ) was
applied to convert all model weights and activations from

integer quantization pipeline was used, with representative
feature vectors provided during calibration. This process gen-

inference hardware. The optimized models were deployed and
evaluated on an NVIDIA Jetson Nano edge device. The Jetson
Nano features a quad-core ARM Cortex-A57 CPU and a 128-
core Maxwell GPU, making it suitable for AI inference at the
edge with moderate power consumption. TensorFlow Lite with
the XNNPACK CPU delegate (INT8) and the GPU delegate
(FP16) was used for runtime execution.
To assess the practicality and performance of the optimized
models on edge hardware, real-time inference testing is per-
formed to ensure the deployed model functions reliably on
the Jetson Nano when presented with live or buffered sensor
inputs. This step verifies the end-to-end system integration,
confirming that the model loads correctly, processes input
data without errors, and generates meaningful outputs under
real-world or simulated operating conditions. Following this,
latency measurement is carried out to quantitatively evaluate
the inference speed of the model. To ensure accurate tim-
ing, inference latency was measured directly on the Jetson
Nano using high-resolution timers within the Python runtime.
The measurement captured the time between input feature
ingestion and classification output, averaged across multiple
inference cycles. All models were evaluated with pre-extracted
sliding window features as input, reflecting realistic deploy-
ment where raw sensor signals are pre-processed prior to
inference. This methodology ensures that deep learning models
originally trained on desktop platforms can be effectively
compressed and deployed on embedded edge hardware such as
the Jetson Nano, enabling intelligent predictive maintenance
directly at the source of data collection.
E. Case Severity Analysis for Decision-Making
To augment fault classification with actionable insight,
we integrate a two-stage pipeline that includes unsupervised
severity estimation of the motor fault. This design enables not
only identification of the fault type but also quantification of its
relative impact, allowing for more informed maintenance plan-
ning. In the first stage, the system performs supervised classifi-
cation using the trained model to detect the fault type based on
input feature vectors, as detailed in III-C. Once a fault is identi-
fied, a second, unsupervised analysis is triggered to determine
its severity. This severity estimation is based on clustering
within a multi-dimensional feature space constructed from the
same time-domain and frequency-domain descriptors used in
the classification model. These include signal energy (RMS),
variability (standard deviation), spectral peaks, crest factor,
and harmonic indicators—features that naturally scale with
fault intensity. Each fault type (e.g., rotor bar defect, stator
fault) is clustered independently to account for differences in
how severity manifests across fault categories. Fig. 4 illustrates
the end-to-end deployment workflow of the proposed edge AI
framework for motor fault diagnosis. Feature vectors computed
from current and vibration streams are transmitted wirelessly
using Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) and MQTT protocols
to a constrained edge device. The optimized model running
on the device first classifies the fault type and subsequently
performs severity clustering. Based on the output, real-time
alerts and actionable insights can be generated for maintenance
or monitoring dashboards.
Fig. 4. Edge AI workflow: enabling on-device fault classification and severity
analysis for real-time alerts.
We employ K-Means clustering due to its low computational
footprint and its suitability for normalized, approximately
spherical data distributions [24]. The algorithm partitions the
historical feature vectors of a particular fault type into three
clusters. The clustering is performed separately for each fault
type, using only historical samples previously labeled with
that fault. This ensures that differences in severity progression
across different fault modes (e.g., rotor vs. stator faults) do
not interfere with intra-fault analysis.
Each cluster discovered within this fault-specific feature
space is then heuristically labeled as Low, Medium, or High
severity, based on the average magnitude of energy-related
features within the cluster centroid. This interpretation is data-
driven rather than hard-coded, allowing flexible adaptation
across deployments. The clustering is performed offline during
model development, and the resulting cluster centroids are

To ensure adaptability in real-world scenarios, the proposed
framework is designed with modularity in mind, allowing
straightforward retraining or fine-tuning on real datasets. Since
the synthetic signals were generated to mimic key physical

IV. EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS
The experimental evaluation was conducted to analyze
the classification accuracy, computational efficiency, and ro-
bustness of all five models under varying noise conditions.
The models were benchmarked on the high-fidelity synthetic
dataset described earlier, across two distinct scenarios: low-
noise conditions and severely noisy industrial settings.
A. Model Performance Under Varying Noise Levels
Under low-noise conditions, all models exhibit strong per-
formance, reflecting reliable classification as shown in Fig.
6. The LSTM model achieved the highest macro F1-score of
96%, demonstrating its ability to leverage temporal dependen-
cies for accurate fault diagnosis. Its gated recurrent structure
allows it to model sequential patterns and subtle variations in
time-series signals, making it highly effective for identifying
both abrupt and gradual fault evolution.
Interestingly, Random Forest (RF) achieved a strong macro
F1-score of 94%, comparable to deep learning models like
RNN (92%), TCN (89%), and GRU (88%). This can be
attributed to the clean signal conditions as in the absence
of noise, the handcrafted statistical features extracted from
the current signals are highly separable across fault classes.
Since RF excels at handling structured, tabular features and
can learn complex non-linear decision boundaries, it performs
competitively when no significant temporal degradation is
present. Furthermore, its ensemble nature helps it generalize
well without overfitting, especially in low-noise environments.
When subjected to severe noise conditions, including ele-
vated jitter, transient drifts, and voltage fluctuations—the clas-
sification performance of all models declined due to increased
signal distortion. However, the LSTM model again demon-
strated superior robustness, maintaining a macro F1-score of
87%, significantly outperforming other models. In contrast,
Random Forest exhibited a sharp decline in performance,
dropping to macro F1-score of 70%. Since RF relies solely
on static statistical features extracted from each window with-
out modeling any temporal context, its discriminative power
degrades significantly when those features become ambiguous
or masked by noise. This highlights a key limitation of non-
temporal models in real-world deployments, where environ-
mental fluctuations are common. Other sequence models like
GRU and TCN also performed moderately well, achieving
macro F1-scores of 80% and 81% respectively as represented
in Fig. 6
Fig. 6. Performance of models under low-noise and severe-noise conditions
The most pronounced performance degradation under severe
noise was observed for mechanical faults, which became
more difficult to distinguish due to overlapping features and
elevated distortion. To further illustrate this, Fig. 7 presents

detection in noisy environments.

B. Model Compression and Embedded Latency Analysis
In this analysis, only the deep learning models (LSTM,
GRU, TCN, and RNN) were subjected to compression and
optimization for embedded deployment. Random Forest was
excluded as it is a tree-based model, and standard compression
techniques such as pruning and quantization are tailored
for differentiable, tensor-based architectures. Due to its non-
differentiable structure and limited compatibility with embed-
ded inference toolchains, RF offers minimal benefits from such
optimization and lacks hardware acceleration support on most
edge devices.

C. Case Severity Analysis Results for Decision-Making
After fault classification, we performed unsupervised sever-
ity assessment using K-Means clustering on historical samples
of each detected fault type. The clustering was conducted inde-
pendently per fault class in a multi-dimensional feature space
combining time-domain (e.g., RMS, kurtosis) and frequency-
domain features (e.g., dominant frequency, harmonic energy
ratios). Each class was partitioned into three severity levels:
low, medium, and high. Although K-Means assumes spherical
cluster shapes, silhouette scores exceeding 0.75 for most fault
types suggest coherent intra-cluster similarity and sufficient
separation. While stator winding faults showed slightly re-
duced cohesion, the overall segmentation effectively reflected
progressive fault behavior. K-Means is selected for its compu-
tational efficiency. However, non-spherical clusters may occur
in practice. Methods such as DBSCAN or Gaussian Mixture
Models could further capture elongated or irregular severity
distributions, which is planned to be explored in future work.
To illustrate, Fig. 8 presents the rotor bar fault clustered in
3D space using acceleration RMS, current standard deviation,
and current RMS. Despite minor visual overlaps, the achieved
silhouette score of 0.77 confirms well-formed clusters in the
full feature space. These results support the feasibility of
integrating severity estimation in real-time fault monitoring
systems.

V. CONCLUSION
This study presents a comprehensive and deployable frame-
work for fault diagnosis and severity assessment in industrial
three-phase induction motors using synthetic high-fidelity sig-
nal modeling, robust machine learning, and embedded system
optimization. An end-to-end framework is introduced from
data generation and feature extraction to model training,
evaluation under varying noise conditions, and real-time de-
ployment on constrained edge devices.
By simulating six critical electromagnetic and mechanical
fault types under both low and severe industrial noise con-
ditions, we demonstrate that sequence-based deep learning
models, particularly LSTM, offer superior performance and
robustness over classical approaches. To enable deployment
in practical industrial environments, we applied structured
pruning and post-training quantization techniques, achieving
significant reductions in model size and inference latency
without substantial loss in accuracy. Our experiments on the
Jetson Nano edge platform confirm the feasibility of real-
time classification under constrained resources. In addition
to classification, a lightweight, unsupervised severity analysis
stage using fault-specific clustering in the time–frequency
feature space is also described. This augmentation enables
more granular, interpretable insights into fault progression,
facilitating condition-based maintenance prioritization. The
full pipeline—covering classification, severity estimation, and
edge deployment—offers a scalable, low-latency solution suit-
able for modern predictive maintenance systems in smart
manufacturing and industrial IoT settings.
Future work will focus on incorporating hybrid signal
sources (e.g., thermal, acoustic) and applying domain adapta-
tion strategies and transfer learning techniques to fine-tune the
current models on real-world data. Since the synthetic setup
closely mimics real motor behavior, the existing models can
be adapted with minimal retraining. It is also aimed to validate
the framework on actual hardware setups to ensure practical
deployment feasibility.
